Transverse Wave Nature of Light

Light is an electromagnetic wave — the electric field (E) and magnetic field (B) oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel. This makes light a transverse wave.

Electric field (E) Magnetic field (B) Direction of travel

Key Points

  • Unlike sound (longitudinal), light does NOT need a medium — it travels through vacuum.
  • In a transverse wave, particle displacement is perpendicular to wave direction.
  • The animation shows E-field in red, B-field in green, both oscillating at 90° to each other and to propagation.

Law of Reflection

When light hits a smooth surface, it reflects. The angle of incidence (i) equals the angle of reflection (r), measured from the normal (a line perpendicular to the surface).

∠i = ∠r   (both measured from the normal)
Angle of incidence (i) = 45°
Angle of reflection (r) = 45°
Verified: i = r ✓

Ray Diagram Checklist

  • Incident ray shown in red, reflected ray in green.
  • Normal line (dashed grey) is perpendicular to surface.
  • Both angles measured from the normal, not from the surface.
  • Rough surface causes diffuse reflection — light scatters in many directions.

Refraction — Snell's Law

When light passes from one medium to another, it bends. The amount of bending depends on the refractive index (n) of each medium.

n = sin(i) / sin(r)     or     n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

Light bends towards the normal when entering a denser medium (higher n), and away from the normal when entering a less dense medium.

n₁ = 1.00 (air), n₂ = 1.33 (water)
i = 40°, r = 28.9°
n = sin(40°) / sin(28.9°) = 1.33 ✓

How to Remember

  • Air → Glass/Water: bends TOWARDS normal (slower in denser medium)
  • Glass/Water → Air: bends AWAY from normal (faster in less dense medium)
  • Frequency stays the same; wavelength and speed change.

Investigating Refraction with Blocks

Click and drag to aim a laser ray at different blocks. Observe how the path changes and measure angles to calculate refractive index.

Shape: Rectangular block | Material: Glass (n = 1.50)

Lateral displacement: visible — ray emerges shifted sideways.

Practical Tips

  • Rectangular block: emergent ray is parallel to incident ray, but shifted sideways.
  • Semi-circular block: ray entering through curved face travels along radius → hits flat face at 0° to normal → no bending at entry!
  • Triangular prism: ray bends twice — can demonstrate dispersion (white light → spectrum).
  • Trace rays on paper, measure i and r with protractor, calculate n = sin(i)/sin(r).

Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

When light travels from a denser to a less dense medium, if the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle (c), all light reflects back — none refracts out.

sin(c) = 1 / n    (where n is refractive index of denser medium)

Critical angle depends only on the material. For glass (n = 1.50), c ≈ 41.8°. For water (n = 1.33), c ≈ 48.8°.

n = 1.50 → critical angle c = 41.8°
Incident angle = 50° > c → TIR occurs!
No refracted ray — 100% reflection.

Applications of TIR

  • Optical fibres: light bounces along the fibre by repeated TIR. Core has higher n than cladding.
  • Prisms: right-angled prisms can reflect light through 90° or 180° with almost no energy loss (better than mirrors!).
  • Sparkle of diamonds: high n (2.42) means small c — light undergoes multiple TIRs before escaping, creating sparkle.
